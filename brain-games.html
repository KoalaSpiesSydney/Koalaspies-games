Why brain games?

Can playing games help your brain as you get older? Research suggests they can help with some kinds of thinking, though they are not a miracle cure.

Dementia Australia explains that the brain can adapt and change at every stage of life, even in later life. Keeping it busy with interesting, challenging and fun activities, such as puzzles, games, reading or learning a language, helps build a "reserve" that supports the brain as we age. Trying something new, a little outside your comfort zone, is especially good for it.

In 2021, researchers pulled together 15 trials involving 759 older adults living in the community, not in care. They found that people who did game-based brain training improved in three areas: short-term memory, focusing on one thing and ignoring distractions, and how quickly they processed information. The improvements were small to moderate. Harder, higher-level skills, such as switching between tasks or holding back an automatic response, did not clearly improve.

The researchers also looked at which kinds of games seemed to work best. The best results came from games with no time pressure, games played with other people, games played on a computer, and games played with some support. Short sessions, up to three times a week and no more than an hour each, were enough. The games on this page can all be played together, taking turns on one screen.

Younger brains respond too. A 2018 study followed 51 healthy young adults, average age about 24. Those who played brain games for about 15 minutes a day for three weeks got better at switching their attention and faster at responding, while a group who didn't play showed no such change. Their memory for patterns did not improve, and the authors say more research is needed to know whether the benefits last.

So brain games won't do everything. As Dementia Australia puts it, there is no guarantee that mental exercise will prevent dementia, but it will keep your mind and brain in their best condition as you get older. Games are an easy, enjoyable way to give your attention and memory a workout, and they work best as one part of a healthy routine alongside sleep, exercise and time with other people. They are not a medical treatment.

The Games

Three to start with. More are on the way.
  • Memory match · 3 levels · 1–3 players

    Colour Idioms

    Play → Feeling blue? Caught red-handed? Turn over two tiles at a time and pair each colour idiom with its meaning. Three levels, from easy to harder, with a bigger board each time. Play solo or with up to three players — most pairs at the end wins.
  • Memory match · Idioms

    Money Idioms

    Play → Costs an arm and a leg? Money doesn't grow on trees? Flip two cards and match each money idiom to what it means. Quick to play, and handy for anyone who talks about prices, work or shopping in English.
  • Idioms · Levels B1 & A1

    Body Idioms

    Play → Everyday English idioms built on the head, hands, feet and more, at two levels. Good practice for idioms you'll actually hear.
  • Coming soon More brain games

How to play

  1. Flip two All the cards start face down. Turn over any two: one might be an idiom, the other a meaning.
  2. Match or remember If the idiom and meaning belong together, they stay face up. If not, they flip back — so remember where they were.
  3. Clear the board Keep going until every pair is found. Playing alone? Try it again and beat your own number of turns.
  4. Play head-to-head Put it on one screen and take turns. Find a pair, go again. Most pairs at the end wins.

About these brain games

The old card game where every card starts face down and you have to remember where you saw its partner — now with a twist. Here you're not matching two pictures, you're matching an idiom to what it really means.

That makes it two workouts at once: memory, to remember where each card is hiding, and meaning, to work out which definition belongs to which expression. Native speakers find it trickier than they expect. English learners pick up phrases they'll actually hear.

Play alone on your phone like a game of patience, or put it on one screen and take turns head-to-head — most pairs wins.

Questions players ask

Can I play the brain games on my own?

Yes. The matching games work solo on a phone, tablet or computer, like a game of patience. They also work head-to-head with friends on one screen.

What level of English do the idiom games need?

They suit intermediate and upper-intermediate learners, and native speakers who like a challenge. Colour Idioms has three levels, from easy to harder.

How do the idiom matching games work?

All the cards start face down. Turn over two at a time and try to pair each idiom with its meaning. Remember where you saw each card to clear the board.

Do I need to sign up or pay?

No. There are no accounts, no subscription and no in-app purchases. Just open a game and play.
